Research note

Short-Lived CVEs

One Patch Tuesday, one Chrome Stable post, and 5,897 CVEs so far this year that shipped with their own fix: what the Windows and Chrome update defaults say about how fast the vulnerable population disappears, how fast fleets actually follow, and the day-zero model that the question behind every Patch Tuesday report needs.

Jerry Gamblin · Head of Research, Empirical Security · October 2, 2026 · CVE data as of October 2, 2026

September's Patch Tuesday carried 965 Microsoft CVEs. Chrome 153 shipped 230 more the same afternoon. By the time anyone had a stable score for any of them, a large share of the machines that mattered had already installed the fix, and that is how both products are built, month after month. This note is about that class of vulnerability, which I have started calling short-lived CVEs, and about what Microsoft and Google actually document for how fast Windows and Chrome update themselves in the three ways most machines are run: standalone, joined to an Active Directory domain, and enrolled in Intune.

TL;DR. A short-lived CVE is one that publishes with its patch, on software that updates itself, so the exposed install base starts shrinking the hour the record exists. Microsoft published 2,962 CVEs through October 2, 2026, 965 of them on September 8 alone; Google's Chrome CNA published 2,935; every one of them fits. Out of the box, Windows has the Patch Tuesday update on disk within about a day and restarts the first time the machine sits idle after hours, with no deadline. Chrome is offered the fix over one to two weeks, downloads it within about five hours of the offer, and runs it on the next relaunch, with no forced relaunch. Joining a domain or enrolling in Intune changes none of that by itself. What changes it is a deadline, and the shorter of Microsoft's two worked examples lands on Friday. Of the 26 Microsoft CVEs from 2026 that reached CISA's KEV list, 16 were added on the day they were published, so anything that is going to say "install this one tonight" has to say it on day zero, from the record itself. That is the model this note argues for: a short-lived CVE score built, like EPSS, from the record's characteristics and from how CVEs like it behaved before, but scored once at publication against a 14-day window and judged on whether it puts the right ten at the top of the release. Microsoft's own exploit-maturity flag already puts 11.7 of this year's 21 Patch Tuesday KEV entries into a top ten; the eleven it did not flag, four of them SharePoint Server, are the problem to solve.

How fast fleets actually follow those defaults: the only public adoption numbers I could find, Microsoft's, cover Windows 10 devices in early 2021 and put the best-run fleets at 92.5% patched on day 14 and unmanaged devices at 73.2%. A fleet with a deadline can be done by Friday. For the population, two weeks is the honest number, and two weeks is still short.

Three caveats up front
  1. These are defaults, not deployments. Every timeline below describes what the software does when nobody changes a setting. A real fleet has WSUS approvals, rings, deferrals, laptops in drawers, and a change board. Defaults are the floor of the argument.
  2. On disk is not running. Chrome stages the new version in the background and keeps running the old one until a relaunch. Windows installs the cumulative update and finishes it on restart. A machine can carry the fix for days and still be running the vulnerable code, so every lane shows both moments.
  3. Vendor documentation and one vendor telemetry post, not my own measurements. The only public, dated adoption numbers I found are Microsoft's, from Windows 10 devices between December 2020 and February 2021. Nothing newer carries both a percentage and a day count. Where I state a number, the source is linked at the bottom.

What I mean by short-lived

Start with the definition. A short-lived CVE has three properties, and it needs all three.

It is born with its patch. The CNA publishes the CVE record at the same moment it publishes the fix. Every one of Microsoft's 2,962 records from 2026 carries exactly one reference, the MSRC Update Guide page, tagged vendor-advisory and patch. Every one of Chrome's 2,935 carries exactly two: the Stable channel post that ships the fixed build and the Chromium issue. There is no window in which the bug is public and the fix is not.

The affected software updates itself. Windows and Chrome both check for updates on a timer, download in the background, and apply the fix with no human in the loop beyond a restart or a relaunch. The default is on. You have to do work to turn it off.

The exposed population collapses in days. Put the first two together and the set of vulnerable machines starts shrinking the hour the CVE exists. That is the property that matters for anyone trying to score or detect it: the thing you are modelling disappears while you are modelling it.

Worth being precise about the contrast. A deserialization bug in a Java library that ships inside a thousand products is long-lived: the CVE is public for months before most of its install base is even identified, let alone fixed. Most vulnerability-management tooling, and most prioritization research, was built for that shape. Short-lived CVEs are the other shape. Microsoft and Chrome together are 5,897 of the 73,483 CVEs published so far in 2026 (about 8%), but on a Windows fleet they are most of the records anyone has to act on in a given week.

Windows installs fast and restarts when you walk away

Microsoft publishes the monthly security update on the second Tuesday, typically at 10:00 AM Pacific, and the CVE records go out in one burst alongside it: 2,639 of the 2,669 Patch Tuesday records this year carry a timestamp within about 25 minutes of 10:00 AM Pacific (09:51 to 10:15). The release note for each update says the same thing every month: "This update downloads and installs automatically from Windows Update and Microsoft Update."

Standalone. The policy reference is unusually plain about the unmanaged default. Updates "are downloaded automatically on non-metered networks and installed during 'Automatic Maintenance' when the device isn't in use and isn't running on battery power." If maintenance cannot run for two days, "Windows Update will install updates right away." Then "the device is automatically restarted when the device isn't actively being used," outside active hours, which are 8 AM to 5 PM in the policy defaults and which Windows can also adjust from how the machine is used. On the scan itself, Microsoft's description of how Windows Update works says only that the client checks "at random intervals." The one number it publishes is for WSUS clients, and it publishes it two ways: the Group Policy page says 22 hours minus up to 20%, the CSP page says 22 hours plus a random 0 to 4 hours. I use "about a day" and nothing finer. What I could not find is a forced restart. The seven-day "deadline before auto-restart" setting is marked legacy and "isn't applicable for Windows 11." A laptop that is in use all day and shut at night can carry the installed update indefinitely. The default is fast when the machine is idle and plugged in, and open-ended when it isn't.

Domain joined, default Group Policy. Joining a domain changes nothing on its own. With the "Specify intranet Microsoft update service location" policy Not Configured, "the Automatic Updates client connects directly to the Windows Update site on the Internet," and the WSUS deployment guide says it outright: "By default, your client computers receive updates from Windows Update. They must be configured to receive updates from the WSUS server instead." What "AD defaults" means in most shops is WSUS, and WSUS is the opposite of a default: "you must approve the updates before they're deployed to the computers on your network." Microsoft deprecated WSUS in September 2024 and still supports it. If an administrator did set the classic option 4 with no time, installs run at 3 AM daily.

Intune, default settings. Enrollment alone changes nothing either: "Devices without a quality update policy continue to receive quality updates through standard Windows Update behavior." The clock changes the moment an update ring carries a deadline. Then "updates are downloaded and installed as soon as they are offered." The effective deadline is the later of the scan time plus the deadline, or the pending restart plus the grace period. "Once the effective deadline is reached, the device is forced to restart regardless of active hours." The user gets fifteen minutes. An Intune ring accepts a quality deadline of 2 to 30 days; Microsoft's Windows 10 guidance on the same page suggests "0 or 1" days with a two-day grace period. The page has two worked examples. One uses three days and says it plainly: "When the update is released on Patch Tuesday, the effective deadline expires on Friday." The other uses seven and lands the following Tuesday. Autopatch's default rollout schedule spaces deferrals and deadlines "from one to 20 days."

Two levers sit on top. Hotpatch means "security updates take effect as soon as they are installed," with no restart, in eight months of the year. Microsoft announced in March 2026 that it would be on by default for eligible Intune-managed devices (Windows 11 24H2 or later, an Enterprise, Education or Business Premium licence, virtualization-based security on) starting with the May 2026 update, unless a quality update policy says otherwise. An expedite policy installs "as soon as possible, bypassing deferral settings" and forces the restart 0, 1 or 2 days after install.

Chrome runs the same clock whether or not anyone manages it

Chrome's Stable channel now ships a new major version every two weeks and a security refresh every week. The CVE records land with the release post. The 230 CVEs for Chrome 153 were published in a 37-second burst starting at 00:09 UTC on September 9, which is 17:09 Pacific on September 8, the same afternoon as Microsoft's Patch Tuesday.

Three facts set the default clock, and none of them moves when you join a domain or enroll in Intune.

The updater checks about every five hours. Google Update wakes hourly and checks for a new build "approximately every 5 hours." The current updater source puts the base period at 4.5 hours, rounded up to the hour, with a one-in-ten chance of stretching to 120% of that. On a system-wide install it runs as a service from boot. On a per-user install it runs from that user's logon.

Google staggers the offer. Every Stable post carries the same line: the build "will roll out over the coming days/weeks." The Chromium release docs say the release "generally reaches all users within one to two weeks unless major issues arise that cannot be addressed quickly." On day zero most machines have not been offered the fixed build yet. Google holds back bug details "until a majority of users are updated."

Download is silent, running it needs a relaunch. Chrome stages the new version in the background and keeps running the old one until the user restarts the browser. With nothing configured there is no prompt. The menu button changes once a third of a seven-day period has passed, changes color at two thirds, and again at seven days. It never forces the relaunch. Forcing exists (set the relaunch notification policy to Required) but it is off.

What management adds is levers. Only domain-joined or MDM-managed machines honor Google Update's machine policy at all, and the shipped default for that policy is "Allow updates (recommended)." The Enterprise MSI installs system-wide and, in Google's words, "overrides any version of Chrome browser installed by a user," which is the one real change: the updater can now check at boot. Google's Intune guide deploys Chrome as a Win32 app from Google's own installer. The guide says nothing about what happens afterwards, but nothing in a Win32 deployment turns Google Update off, so it keeps updating the browser. Worth checking on your own tenant: if the detection rule pins a version (a file version, a registry version, or the MSI product version check), Intune will try to reinstall the uploaded version every time Chrome updates itself. Microsoft's line-of-business app docs name Chrome as the self-updating example and call that outcome a race condition; the same logic applies to a version-checking Win32 detection rule.

Google wrote it down itself in July: "the time spent waiting for the user to restart Chrome can be a significant contributor to N-day exploitation risk." The default Chrome clock is roughly five hours from offer to download, one to two weeks for the offer to reach the whole population, and then however long the user keeps the window open.

Fleets follow in about two weeks, by the only public numbers

The only public adoption telemetry I could find with both a percentage and a day count is a Microsoft post from July 2021, covering "the many tens-of-millions of devices that are sending telemetry" between December 2020 and February 2021, on Windows 10. It splits devices into three groups and reports the share fully patched at 14 and 28 days after release.

Worth being precise about what those numbers are: device shares, on Windows 10, in early 2021. By that measure the best-run fleets are above 90% at two weeks. Unmanaged devices are about three quarters patched at two weeks. The WSUS-style shops, the ones most people picture when they say "domain joined," are barely half.

Day three is not published anywhere. The post describes the unmanaged segment as devices "used on an irregular schedule, often offline, and hard to update," and the defaults above fill in the mechanics: off, on battery, on a metered connection, never idle long enough to restart, or paused by the user, which Windows 11 allows for up to five weeks. No default forces any of them.

That cohort also predates Windows 11, scan-based deadlines, and hotpatch, so it is a floor on today's speed, not a measurement of it. Two things have moved since 2021, both in the same direction. Microsoft's March 2026 hotpatch post says the four customers it asked, each with 30,000 to 70,000 devices, "all reported achieving 90% patch compliance in half the previous time," and that "Typically, you'd allow 3-5 days after installing those fixes before forcing a restart to apply them." It does not say what the previous time was. And the shorter of Microsoft's two worked deadline examples is Tuesday to Friday. For a managed fleet with a deadline, Friday is real. For the population, two weeks is the honest number, and it is still short.

The same 2021 post is where Microsoft writes that for a severe CVE "we have seen live exploits released within 48 hours by bad actors." That is the race: the exploit at day two, the managed fleet forced on day three, and the long tail of everyone else fading out over a fortnight.

The question behind every Patch Tuesday report

When a customer asks for a Patch Tuesday report, they are not asking which of the 965 CVEs are severe. For the Windows and Office CVEs among them, the defaults above plus a deadline will have the fix installed by Friday, and the population will get there in about two weeks without anyone reading a report. The question is narrower and harder: which of these do I need to move faster than my automated patching? Which ten do I push tonight, out of cycle, with a restart during active hours, because waiting for Friday is the wrong bet?

Severity cannot answer it. On September 8, 489 of the 965 carried a CVSS of 7.8 or higher and 43 scored 9.0 or higher; Tenable counted 104 that Microsoft rated Critical. None of those is a list anyone can act on tonight.

The KEV set does not separate on severity anyway. The median CVSS of the 26 Microsoft CVEs that reached KEV this year is 7.8, the same as the median of the other 2,936. Scoring 9.0 or above helps a little, 19% of the KEV set against 7% of the rest, and not nearly enough.

What day zero already knows

The best day-zero signal is already in the record, and it is the vendor's. Microsoft's CVE records carry a CVSS temporal vector, and when Microsoft has seen a working exploit the vector says E:F, functional. Since the start of 2024, 64 Microsoft CVEs carry that flag in the record today and 52 of them (81%) are on KEV. Proof-of-concept (E:P) runs 4.1%; unproven (E:U), which is 5,121 of the 5,301 records, runs 0.6%. This year the flag covers 9 of the 16 same-day KEV additions. CISA listed the other seven on day zero even though the CVE record's vector, as it stands today, marks them unproven; I did not check the Security Update Guide's own Exploited field, which is the primary signal the vector is derived from. One caveat I cannot remove: the record I can read is the current one, and Microsoft revises these vectors. Nine of the 52 flagged KEV entries were listed between 1 and 21 days after publication, which hints that some flags were set after the fact, so treat the flag's numbers as an upper bound on what it knew on day zero. Any model worth building still starts by trusting it.

EPSS does not answer the question either, and it was not built to. It predicts exploitation activity over the next 30 days across every CVE, and by day 30 a Windows fleet is mostly patched. Of the nine Microsoft CVEs this year that reached KEV between day 2 and day 126, 7 have an EPSS score in my snapshot cache from the day after publication, or the first snapshot within a week. One was above 0.1: CVE-2026-50522, a SharePoint remote code execution bug, at 0.197 and the 97th percentile. The median of the seven was 0.005, and their percentiles ran from the 12th to the 97th. Full disclosure: Empirical Security, where I work, generates the EPSS scores for the FIRST EPSS SIG, so read that number knowing where I sit. EPSS answers a different question, and that number says so.

Of the 21 Patch Tuesday CVEs that reached KEV this year, 10 carry Microsoft's functional-exploit flag in the record. The other 11 do not, and they are the whole problem. Read down the component column. Four are SharePoint Server, two of them deserialization bugs, and SharePoint remote code execution across all of Microsoft's title variants has put 8 of its 64 CVEs since 2024 on KEV (12.5%, against a base rate of 1.7% for everything Microsoft publishes). One is the WinSock ancillary function driver, which has 4 KEV entries in 56 CVEs. These are not random draws from the 2,962. They look like CVEs that reached KEV before, in the same component, with the same kind of weakness, and that is the premise of the model this note is arguing for: score a new CVE by how CVEs like it behaved, before anyone has seen this one exploited.

Now read the last column. Seven of the eleven reached KEV inside 14 days, the window this note cares about. The four that took 35 to 126 days are three SharePoint Server CVEs and one IKE bug. SharePoint Server is the one product in this table that an administrator patches by hand, on a server, on the organization's schedule. Its vulnerable install base survives for months rather than weeks, which is a good candidate reason its KEV entries arrive late. A short-lived CVE model would count the seven. The other four are on a different clock, and the model should know which clock a CVE is on.

I tried the dumbest version first

Before arguing for a model I built the version that fits in a spreadsheet. For every Microsoft CVE from 2024 and 2025, take the share of CVEs in the same component with the same impact type that reached KEV, shrunk toward the impact-level rate when the component has little history. Score each of the nine Patch Tuesday releases of 2026 with it, take the top ten per release, and count how many of the 21 KEV entries landed in those top tens. Three baselines sit beside it: a random order, CVSS, and Microsoft's flag alone; the lookup table is scored with and without the flag. CVSS has ties by the dozen at every score that matters, so every number is an average over 200 random tie-breaks.

So the spreadsheet version does not earn its keep. I am writing that down because it is the honest floor for the real thing, and because it says what the real thing has to do better.

What the model has to be

A short-lived CVE model is a day-zero prior. It takes only what exists the moment the record publishes and answers one question: how likely is this CVE to show exploitation evidence inside the window before the defaults finish the job? It is a probability, and it is judged by where it puts each CVE within its own release.

  • Scored once, at publication, and ranked within the release. The output is the list for Tuesday night, and it does not drift the next morning. The unit that matters is "the ten out of 965".
  • A label window tied to the product's patch clock. For Windows and Chrome, exploitation evidence inside 14 days of publication (KEV, the vendor's own flag changing, telemetry), because that is the window in which the install base is still there to be exploited. For a server product an administrator patches by hand, the window is longer, and the model should carry that distinction rather than average over it.
  • Trained on CVEs like it. Vendor, component, weakness, attack vector and privilege required, the vendor's exploitability and disclosure flags, who reported it, whether it shipped alone or inside a bundle of hundreds, and how each of those cohorts behaved in past releases. The spreadsheet test says component alone is not enough: the weakness and the vector have to carry weight, the history has to span more than two years, and the shrinkage has to be honest about five-record components.
  • Judged against the baselines above. Hits in the top ten per release against CVSS and against the vendor flag, and recall on the unflagged set. If it cannot beat the flag on the whole set and CVSS on the unflagged eleven, it is a spreadsheet.
  • Honest about the floor. KEV is a floor on exploitation, so every miss may be a real exploit nobody listed, and some of the unflagged eleven may not be predictable from the record at all. The model should state its ceiling.

That is what a Patch Tuesday report should be: a short list with a reason beside each entry ("SharePoint remote code execution, 8 of 64 on KEV since 2024"), the vendor's flag where it exists, and one sentence saying the defaults will take care of the other 955 by Friday for a managed fleet and by two weeks for everyone else.

If you defend a Windows fleet

The defaults are already fast for a machine that goes idle. The thing that turns "eventually" into "Friday" is a deadline, and the shorter of Microsoft's two worked examples is three days. For Chrome the equivalent is a single policy, RelaunchNotification set to Required, and without it the fix sits on disk until someone closes the browser. Check the Intune detection rule on Chrome while you are there.

If you build scoring models

Separate the regimes. For a short-lived CVE the useful prediction is made once, on day zero, from record characteristics, and the observation window closes around day 14 for the populations that matter. Start from the vendor's own flag, beat the baselines in the table above before claiming anything, and treat late exploitation signal on these as what it is: signal about stragglers.

If you write Patch Tuesday reports

Lead with the handful that should not wait for the normal cycle and say why, then say that the rest will be handled by the defaults and a deadline. The count of CVEs is not the finding. The ten are.

Methodology and Reproducibility

CVE counts. CVE List V5 (the cvelistV5 GitHub clone) as processed by the CVEGraphs pipeline, non-rejected records, assigner short names microsoft and Chrome, grouped by the record's datePublished. Patch Tuesday is derived as the second Tuesday of the month, not hand-listed. KEV is CISA's JSON catalog, 1,731 entries at collection; lags are calendar-day differences between the KEV dateAdded and the CVE publication date, both normalized to dates. Data collected October 2, 2026 (CVE List snapshot 14:13 UTC); vendor documentation checked the same day.

Backtest. src/cvegraphs/papers/short_lived_cves/backtest_lookalike.py in the same repo. Every Microsoft-assigned record published 2024 to 2026 (5,301) is parsed from the cvelistV5 clone for the CVSS temporal exploit-maturity flag (E:U, E:P, E:F) and for Microsoft's title, which gives the component (the title with the impact phrase removed) and the impact type. KEV labels come from the catalog released October 1, 2026, whenever added, and the exploit-maturity flag is read from the record as it stands in the clone, not as it stood at publication. The lookup score for a 2026 CVE is (KEV count + 5 × impact-level rate) / (records + 5) over the 2024 to 2025 records with the same component and impact, falling back to the impact-level rate for unseen components. "Flag" ranks E:F first, then E:P. Ties, which CVSS has in the hundreds, break at random; the table reports the mean over 200 seeds. Hits count KEV entries in the top 10 or top 25 of each of the nine Patch Tuesday releases, summed. EPSS scores are taken from the repo's daily EPSS snapshot cache on the day after publication, or the first snapshot within a week; the cache holds only CVEs that later reached KEV, which is why EPSS cannot be ranked against a whole release here, and two of the nine late entries have no snapshot inside that week.
